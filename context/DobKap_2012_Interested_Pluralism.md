**Leonhard Dobusch and Jakob Kapeller**  
*Journal of Economic Issues*, Vol. XLVI No. 4, December 2012, pp. 1035–1057. DOI: 10.2753/JEI0021-3624460410

## Core Claim

Pluralism is the key term of heterodox self-understanding, yet its nature and role remain contested. The paper closes this conceptual gap by proposing **"interested pluralism"**: replace the traditional demarcation criteria between economic traditions with a set of **ecumenical pluralist principles** — a consciously formed **"pluralist (meta-)paradigm"** that is not merely possible but desirable, both as a framework for organising heterodox research and as a **single unified competitor** to neoclassical economics.

## Motivation and Terminology

- The rise of neoclassical dominance has been matched by a growing literature on economic pluralism (Rothschild 1999: a plurality of paradigms is "not only an obvious fact but also a necessary and desirable phenomenon"; Garnett, Olsen and Starr 2010). Garnett et al. distinguish **two waves**: the first (1970s–early 1980s) gathered largely mutually disinterested heterodox schools; the second (Fullbrook 2009; Marqués and Weisman 2010) engages integrative, post-Kuhnian notions. Turning point: the Hodgson, Mäki and McCloskey (1992) petition in the *American Economic Review*, signed by 44 leading economists, calling for "a new spirit of pluralism in economics, involving critical conversation and tolerant communication between different approaches".
- Reform calls intensified after the global financial crisis shattered neoclassical prestige and dissent grew inside the mainstream (Colander et al. 2004).
- Terminology: **"neoclassical"** = the dominating ("orthodox") core theory of current **"mainstream"** economics (commitment varies, weakest at Colander et al.'s "edge of the mainstream"); **"heterodox"** = a collection of non-neoclassical schools, "neither fully consistent nor easily definable". This conception explains "grey areas" (Dow 2000) and is compatible with competing readings (Colander et al. 2007–2008; Dequech 2007–2008; Vernengo 2010; Lavoie 2009; Backhouse 2004 as conceptual basis; cf. Dobusch and Kapeller 2009). Figure 1 maps the discourse: Neoclassical Orthodoxy and Mainstream Dissenters ("Colander's Edge", behavioural economists) inside the mainstream; Post-Keynesians, feminist, ecological, institutionalist, evolutionary economists and Austrians as Heterodox Dissenters.
- Problem: meta-level engagement with paradigmatic pluralism (the diversity of the Garnett et al. volume shows it) has **not** yielded compatible suggestions for the pluralistic conduct of research. Institutional characteristics like citation-based evaluation (Dobusch and Kapeller 2009; Kapeller 2010a) may or may not fit various pluralisms, and the Kuhnian vs post-Kuhnian gap persists (Mearman 2008 special issue). Hence the need for a framework for pluralism **in research praxis, independent of paradigmatic background** — appealing to all dissatisfied with the institutional and conceptual dominance of neoclassical economics (not necessarily with its content).
- Roadmap: reconcile Kuhnian paradigms with post-Kuhnian pluralism via a descriptive reading of "paradigm" (next section); survey the potential of pluralist thinking and dominant approaches; go beyond "another call for pluralism" by sketching the conscious formation of a pluralist framework; give its conceptual foundation; conclude.

## Paradigm: A Working Definition

- The Kuhnian account connects "paradigm" to the sociology of scientific fields but is clouded by conceptual ambiguity (Masterman [1970] counts 21 usage categories in Kuhn). Either the term is vacuous, or its diffusion signals a series of distinct but connected aspects of scientific fields as special forms of social organisation.
- The authors therefore **strip "paradigm" of its epistemological connotations** (e.g., incommensurability) and use it descriptively: scientists from different traditions use distinct terminologies (which may explain lack of understanding). The existence of a paradigm has **social, rather than logical, implications** — a positive term in social analysis, merging Kuhn with the sociology of knowledge (Berger and Luckmann 1966; Gouldner 1970; Fleck [1935] 1979's "thought-collective").
- A scientific paradigm entails a theoretical perspective (ontological and teleological aspects). Researchers sharing it embrace four "styles of thought" dimensions and three "thought collective" dimensions:

| Dimension | Content |
|-----------|---------|
| Axiomatic | A series of theoretical propositions |
| Metaphorical | Related images, heuristics, important individuals |
| Practical | A certain set of archetypical applications |
| Terminological | Theory-specific concepts |
| Institutional | Shared respected institutions (conferences, associations, journals) |
| Methodological | Basic methodological requirements / typically applied methods |
| Evaluative | Conceptions of academic standards (quality, originality, empirical robustness) |

- Shared analytical categories give rise to specific **institutional routines** — emergent from shared "styles of thought", constituted as social mechanisms (institutions, informal codes of conduct). Different paradigms **may but need not** be non-commensurable (different notions of theory, quality, evidence).
- The notion of **paradigm shift is relaxed**: no instantaneous simultaneous revolution across all dimensions is required; incremental, evolutionary change is allowed. But if paradigmatic developments mimic **path-dependent processes** (Dobusch and Kapeller 2009; Sterman and Wittenberg 1999), occasional rapid shifts in one or more dimensions are no surprise.
- **Table 1** applies the scheme to three economic paradigms (illustrative, not "the most important"; dissenting traditions are diverse — ordoliberalism, French regulation school, Austrians, radical economists, economic geography, parts of behavioural economics):

| Paradigmatic aspect | Neoclassical paradigm | Evolutionary paradigm | Post-Keynesian paradigm |
|---------------------|-----------------------|-----------------------|-------------------------|
| Central problem | Scarcity | Change | Unemployment |
| Solution (teleological) | Efficiency | Development | Full employment |
| Level of analysis (ontological) | Micro (individual actors) | Meso (institutions, emergent phenomena, interactive processes) | Macro (aggregates, money) |
| Actor conceptions | Optimizing rationality | Creativity and strategic rationality | Animal spirits and procedural rationality |
| Central property of markets | Equilibrium (Say's Law) | Creative destruction | Effective demand |
| Archetypical science | Physics (classical mechanics) | Biology (evolutionary theory) | Engineering (technical solutions) |
| Archetypical individual | P. A. Samuelson | J. A. Schumpeter | J. M. Keynes |
| Typical applications | Popular models (rational actor, perfect competition) | Research on innovation | Economic policy (fiscal and monetary) |
| Idiosyncratic terms | "marginality" | "new combination" | "(fundamental) uncertainty" |
| Important institutions | AER, QJE, JPE…; AEA, EEA… | JEE, ICC, JEI…; AFEE, EAEPE… | JPKE, CJE…; URPE |
| Dominant methodologies | Formal modelling / econometrics | Simulations and case studies | Formal modelling / econometrics / historical studies |
| Quality criteria | Model-building methodology, significance tests | Focus on micro/meso processes, problem-specific methodologies | Model-building methodology, significance tests, policy relevance |

- Such comparison is a conceptual guideline for **commensurability** questions: Do paradigms relate to distinct or similar objects? Do viewpoints/policy implications conflict, complement, or coincide? Can terminologies be translated into each other? Necessary both at specific scale and for progress in economic thought.

## Pluralism and Paradigms

- The pluralism literature struggles to integrate traditions under a common umbrella. Proposed common cornerstones of heterodox thought: ontological reflection (Bigo and Negru 2008; Caldwell 2004; Lawson 2006, 2010; Nelson 2003), methodological similarities (Dow 2004, 2008), opposition to mainstream economics (Colander et al. 2004; Dequech 2007–2008) or to capitalism (Lee 2010).
- Dequech (2007–2008, 279) summarises: heterodox economics "can be defined negatively, in opposition to … the mainstream. … Another possibility would be to define heterodox economics positively, but the result in the current period may be an **empty set**." The same holds for "pluralist economics".
- Proposal: a **"pluralist (meta-)paradigm"** synthesises the conceptual and methodological diversity of dissenting approaches into a common research strategy — a single competitor to neoclassical economics that "would need neither a common core nor a shared enemy": **the principle of pluralism itself is the decisive connecting feature**. Paradigm-specific idiosyncrasies (as in Table 1) are consciously and successively replaced by pluralist principles — non-dogmatic, ecumenical, and quality-assuring.
- Against monist dissenters: Boettke (2007) rejects pluralism "at the level of the individual scientist"; the post-Keynesian identity debate (triggered by King's 2002 history) shows monist tendencies — Davidson's reading of Keynes (rejection of money neutrality, gross substitutability, ergodicity) is widely welcomed, but his claim of the *General Theory* as an all-encompassing theory "similar to the theory of relativity" is "astonishing" (King 2005b) and an "extraordinary claim" (Hodgson 2005). Lesson: "if you [dogmatically] believe in the correctness of your ideas, you don't want pluralism; you want your ideas to win out because they are correct" (Colander et al. 2007–2008) — **dogmatism and pluralism are fundamentally opposed** (Dow 2004).

### Reasons for Pluralism

Three rough categories from the literature (research: Fullbrook 2009; Garnett et al. 2010; teaching: Freeman 2010; Groenewegen 2007; Reardon 2009):

- **Epistemological**: science aims at knowledge superior to religion or gossip; it must engage in "sorting" between better and worse explanations while all explanations stay fallible. Fallibility excludes dogmatism; serious evaluation demands competing explanations be **more or less evenly represented** (Albert 1985; Mill [1859] 2001; Popper [1935] 2004; Mearman's "theoretical pluralism"). Current economics fails this: citation analysis shows mainstream journals systematically ignore heterodox contributions (Cronin 2010; Dobusch and Kapeller 2012; Kapeller 2010a, 2010b; Lee 2004) — motivating "academic freedom" (Garnett 2011) and academic tolerance (Lee 2011a).
- **Ontological**: researchers' preconceptions about matter, mind and reality shape what they perceive; social reality is multi-faceted and requires a **variety of perspectives** (Norgaard 1989; Samuels 1998; Dow's "Babylonian" approach). Giere (1999): different "maps" for different purposes (hiking, driving, steering a boat). Seemingly competing theories may be **neutral or complementary**, simply answering different questions — a blind struggle for a "single best answer" compares apples with oranges. Ontological sensitivity is required to differentiate better from worse explanations.
- **Methodological**: the plethora of methods in social research shows diversification's potential; every research question is unique and the question should determine the method (Bigo 2010; Dow 2008; Lawson 2004; Norgaard 1989; Samuels 1998) — though in practice researchers pick from existing methodological blueprints rather than starting afresh.

### Varieties of Pluralism

Three basic approaches, differing in attitude to inter-paradigmatic controversy on (a) discursive and (b) teleological levels (Table 2):

| Conception of pluralism | Discourse level | Teleological level |
|-------------------------|-----------------|--------------------|
| Selfish | Regular critique | Dominance and/or survival |
| Disinterested | Occasional critique and/or engagement | Containment of status quo |
| Interested | Constructive engagement | Ecumenical integration and diversification |

- **Selfish pluralism** (a.k.a. "strategic pluralism", De Langhe 2010; Sent 2003; van Bouwel 2005): pluralism as a **transitional solution** — a rhetorical vehicle to ensure the survival of one's preferred paradigm; other dissenters are "temporary allies" against the mainstream, ultimately still competing paradigms (criticised by Davis 1997; Garnett 2006; Holcombe 2008; van Bouwel 2005).
- **Disinterested pluralism** ("plurality" in Bigo and Negru 2008; "tolerance" in Mearman 2011): analytical **coexistence** of traditions — as in sociology or management studies (and, per Morgan and Rutherford 1998, interwar economics): colleagues interact within their own tradition, others are tolerated and only occasionally criticised; no ambition for interaction, integration, or disciplinary conquest.
- **Interested pluralism**: striving for **constructive interaction** between traditions to arrive at an improved and expanded set of explanatory statements — intensified inter-paradigmatic awareness and respect (Hopkins 2010), theoretical integration (Lavoie 2006; O'Hara 2007, 2008), or a sole/unified paradigmatic competitor to neoclassical economics (Dobusch and Kapeller 2009; Lee 2010), including identifying relative strengths, weaknesses and complementarities.
- Conceptual difference: "disinterested" pluralism suits fields without dominant paradigms under a disciplinary umbrella; "interested" pluralism can unite approaches under a common umbrella — generating "not a 'pluralism of paradigms,' but a '**pluralist paradigm**'".
- Why interested pluralism is the viable choice for heterodox economics: (1) it is most compatible with all the epistemological/ontological/methodological rationales, which hinge on **conscious interaction**; (2) **power differentials**: formal results (Sterman and Wittenberg 1999) show the number of competitors is the most decisive variable for a dominant paradigm's survival — the more fragmented dissenters are, the more easily the dominant paradigm sustains itself; this matches Lavoie's (2006, 2009) description of fragmented heterodox practice; (3) heterodox economists mostly practise selfish or disinterested pluralism — citation analysis shows heterodox traditions cite many mainstream works while mainstream articles neglect heterodox voices; the mainstream practises an "internal pluralism" with diversified sources, while cross-school interaction is rare among heterodox scholars, leaving heterodox citation networks **porous** compared to the tight mainstream network (Dobusch and Kapeller 2012; Kapeller 2010a; Lee 2008, 2009).
- Consequence: citation-based quality assessment further strengthens mainstream power over publication outlets and funding. Given rising journal rankings in evaluation, **strengthening interaction among dissenting economists is a *conditio sine qua non* for long-term academic survival**. The imperative: create interested pluralism (a pluralist paradigm), not disinterested pluralism (a pluralism of paradigms).

## What Is a Pluralist Paradigm?

> "If this sounds like openness, eclecticism, and tolerance for ambiguity, I plead guilty. The alternative is premature closure, myopic determinism, and sectarianism." — Samuels (2000, 312)

- Paradigms are, in the last resort, **social constructions** (Berger and Luckmann 1966) — man-made pillars helping scientists describe, categorise and evaluate events. That is exactly why consciously re-evaluating our paradigmatic stance (heavy with preconceptions and prejudices) makes sense. The aim: reflect and eventually **reverse** these presuppositions, replacing narrow tradition-specific preconceptions with an ecumenical conception of science where mutual understanding has priority — providing "a core set of principles by which to guide practice" (Dow 2008). In this skeletal sense a paradigm is just a framework — "a device for conceptual exploration".
- **Table 3** (the framework): take the dimensional characteristics of a typical paradigm as given and replace tradition-specific idiosyncrasies with general principles — a level playing field of shared preconceptions for constructive mutual engagement:

| Dimension | Preconceptions offered by a pluralist paradigm |
|-----------|------------------------------------------------|
| Central problem | Explaining and illuminating the economy / economic activity / socio-economic processes |
| Solution (teleological) | Explanations with increasing empirical success and precision; in short "truth" or the "elimination of error" |
| Level of analysis (ontological) | Ontological tolerance: awareness of the multi-facetness of social reality, implying a manifoldness of analytical perspectives |
| Conceptions of actors and institutions (axiomatic) | Acceptance of competing hypotheses that may be true or false or simply applicable to different social contexts; thorough demarcation of scopes of application |
| Archetypical social setting (metaphorical) | Ideal speech situation as envisaged by Habermas (1981) |
| Typical applications (practical) | Integration of theoretical propositions, diversification of applications/methods in new contexts, comparisons of theories/methods/results across traditions (a division of labour), excavation of ontological/teleological stances, evaluation of competing theoretical statements |
| Important institutions (institutional) | WEA, ICAPE, AHE, FAPE and ROPE, JEI, AJES, PAER/RWER as archetypes of emerging associations and journals |
| Dominant methodology | Concrete methods justified against the specific problem; no method superior per se |
| Quality criteria (evaluative) | Content-oriented evaluation instead of citation counting; contribution to integration of heterodox thought as quality criterion; ecumenical peer review |

- Elaborations:
  - The framework must eschew theoretical presuppositions — an **agnostic** basic perspective: economics as analysis of economic phenomena in the broadest sense (cf. Lee's 2011b "social-provisioning process"). Teleological and axiomatic descriptions are deliberately general (descriptive and evaluative work allowed) but signal **slight hostility to purely speculative model-building** with unrealistic, empirically unreflective assumptions (Hausman 1992; Sugden 2000; Rubinstein 2006): thought experiments grounded in idealised conceptions are inferior to assumptions grounded in empirically defendable arguments.
  - **Methodological level**: methods must be justified against the research subject — aligned with methodological pluralism (Dow 2004, 2008; Samuels 1998) but explicitly **not** Feyerabend's (1977) "anything-goes".
  - **Ontological level**: agnostic, balanced position (Lawson 2006, 2010; Nelson 2003) — sensibility, awareness and tolerance as conditions for communicating across traditional boundaries (Bigo and Negru 2008; Samuels 1998).
  - **Metaphorical level**: replace the disciplinary archetypes of Table 1 with Habermas's (1981) **ideal speech situation** — every competent "voice" may take part, ask questions, introduce assertions, express attitudes, free from internal or external coercion. This implies a "truly substantive, or deep pluralism": not only theoretical and methodological pluralism but "greater diversity in the people who participate in scholarly conversations" and "in the topics of investigation" (Strassmann et al. 2010).
  - **Institutional and evaluative levels**: institutional characteristics emerge over time and depend on historical idiosyncrasies; Table 3 therefore lists **role models** (WEA, ICAPE, AHE, FAPE; ROPE, JEI, AJES, and the Real-World Economics Review, formerly Post-Autistic Economics Review) rather than prescriptions.
- The framework resolves Garnett's (2006) vexing questions — "What exactly do we stand for as heterodox economists? … Are we paradigm warriors, first and foremost? Or are we pluralists…?" One can answer "yes" to both (if pluralism itself is the prize the paradigm warrior fights for, the tension dissolves) while the framework gives clear answers on intellectual priorities **without imposing any specific theoretical or methodological view**.
- **Not relativism or postmodernism** (Bigo and Negru 2008; De Langhe 2010; Dow 2008), on three counts: (1) epistemologically, "not anything goes" — pluralism "must respect logic, consistency and the stability of meanings within arguments" (Marqués and Weismann 2008) and proposes a systematic search for knowledge; (2) ontological awareness does not mean accepting propositions uncritically, but meeting the prerequisites for understanding different foundations; (3) tolerating alternative hypotheses needs no relativism — **fallibilism** (any statement, including one's own, may be wrong) suffices for tolerant discourse. Politeness and mutual respect are hidden premises. The mixed armada of diverse craft should navigate "the Scylla of the bonds of the modernist, mechanical worldview and the Charybdis of the relativism and subjectivism of the deconstructionist alternative" (Nelson 2003).

### Relation to the current mainstream

- The framework is by definition **opposed to the paradigmatic dominance** of neoclassical economics but by its nature **incorporates neoclassical approaches**: it accepts neoclassical research within its boundaries (Samuels 1998) while rejecting the institutional and theoretical dominance of any single approach — potentially acceptable to heterodox and mainstream dissenters alike. The "distortions caused by using models centered around the choice behaviour of individual, autonomous, self-interested agents" (Nelson 2003) are primarily a result of **ontological monism**; within a pluralist setting such approaches can be complemented or challenged — not precluded a priori.
- One required change in heterodox practice: heterodox journals profusely cite mainstream journals while largely ignoring heterodox counterparts outside their own sub-group (Kapeller 2010a, 2010b). Recommendation: **devote less time to (orthodox) mainstream economics and more to other dissenting traditions** — a strategic imperative given citation rankings: "Heterodoxy must create a tighter citation network, or risk being simply 'outranked' in the very near future." Neoclassical economics should get as much attention as any other approach — not disproportionally more.
- Embedding neoclassical economics: Colander, Garnett and co-authors (Colander et al. 2004, 2007–2008, 2010; Garnett 2006) and Fontana and Gerrard (2006) emphasise that inter-school dialogue should be polite and at least partly constructive (for critical appraisals see Stockhammer and Ramskogler 2009; Lavoie 2009; Vernengo 2010). From a pluralist stance there is no basis for a priori exclusion or degradation of neoclassical arguments (though Dow 2008; Lawson 2006; Lee 2010 propose as much — oxymoronically); not all elements of neoclassical thought are wrong, dangerous, or inapplicable (King 2012). Dissenting ideas **can** influence orthodox practice — e.g., the Taylor rule in monetary policy (central banks control the interest rate instead of the money supply; King 2012; Lavoie 2009) — most effectively when delivered politely, constructively, and in a facilitating language. A "Colanderian" discourse at the level of economic policy (Colander et al. 2010) may prove more fruitful than invoking fundamental theoretical debates.

## Practicing Pluralism: Integration, Diversification, Comparison, and Exchange

- "Interested pluralism" implies the **integration of different schools of thought** (Bigo and Negru 2008). Following Lee (2010, 19) that pluralism means "engagement across different heterodox approaches", this engagement is most productively realised **at the level of theoretical statements dealing with distinct empirical phenomena** — not abstract paradigm comparison — allowing theory comparison while empirical work on real-world problems continues.
- **Table 4** relates the type of relation between theoretical statements from different traditions to the pluralist research practice it invites:

| Comparison between theoretical statements | Pluralist research practice |
|-------------------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| (1) Identical | (a) Integration |
| (2) Convergent | (a) Integration |
| (3) Compatible | (b) Division of labor |
| (4) Neutral | (b) Division of labor |
| (5) Divergent | (c) Diversification |
| (6) Contradictory | (d) Test of conflicting hypotheses |

  (In the original layout, practice (a) spans relations (1)–(2), (b) spans (3)–(4), (c) diversification is reached via recombination from neutral and divergent statements, and (d) covers divergent and contradictory statements operationalised empirically.)

- **(a) Integration** (identical or convergent statements): Waller (2010) — "different strands of economic thought borrow from one another all the time" — cites mere terminological differences between foundational works of feminist economics (Gilman 1898/1994) and institutional economics (Veblen [1899] 1934, [1894] 1964). Lavoie (1992): institutional economics could provide the **microfoundations** of post-Keynesian economics (integration via complementarity). O'Hara (2007, 2008) lists linkages between convergence sub-schools of institutional-evolutionary political economy ("post-Keynesian institutionalists", "Schumpeterians", "socio-economic institutionalists"). A further foundation: comparing similar concepts across traditions — e.g., "irreversibility of actions" in evolutionary/post-Keynesian (Davidson 1991), ecological (Daly and Farley 2004) and Austrian economics (Shackle 1966) — where a thorough comparison of what each framework means by the phrase is still missing and would decide whether the conceptions are identical or convergent.
- **(b) Division of labor** (compatible or neutral statements): explicitly suggested by Bigo and Negru 2008; Lawson 2006, 2010; O'Hara 2008. Lawson (2010): heterodox traditions "are identifiable more by the sorts of questions asked … we can view the separate traditions as divisions of labor" — post-Keynesianism (fundamental uncertainty), institutionalism (processual social reality), feminist economics (position of women and marginalised groups). For neutral (unrelated) statements: John King (2008) notes all available heterodox short-period macro models are post-Keynesian except the Goodwin (1967) trade cycle model — so until superior theories arrive (e.g., Flaschel 2009 integrating Schumpeterian, Marxian and Keynesian ideas on Goodwin's basis), all heterodox approaches can adopt post-Keynesian short-period theory; some traditions (institutionalist, feminist) have **no macro theory at all**. Keen (1995) applies evolutionary simulation techniques to Minsky's financial instability hypothesis — an evolutionary methodology illustrating a post-Keynesian argument. Different traditions are often "much more complementary than they are competitive".
- **(c) Diversification** (via recombination of neutral and divergent statements): the Taylor rule — a competing Keynesian thesis complements the neoclassical standard model, enhancing its plausibility — is a neat example of recombination, hence diversification of post-Keynesian thought (criticisable as incomplete, but real). Lavoie (2009) flags a blind spot: post-Keynesian thought neglects ecological approaches —

  > "Obviously, some work is also needed to reconcile the post-Keynesian target of full-employment with the requirements of ecological economics and its call for slower or zero economic growth. So far, post-Keynesians have written very little on the subject of the environment."

  Integrating post-Keynesian and ecological growth positions is hard (divergent statements), but thorough investigation may yield diversification or a clearer picture of presuppositions (Kronenberg 2010; for Marxist vs ecological economics see Burkett 2004; Nelson 2001).
- **(d) Test of conflicting hypotheses** (divergent or contradictory statements): de Langhe (2010) describes "antagonistic pluralism" where viewpoints are always opposing, neglecting constructive integration — but an "agreement to disagree" can be productive if claims are clearly antagonistic and **put to an empirical test**. The authors argue for acknowledging antagonistic relationships where two statements are demonstrably contradictory and evidence can guide fair judgment — while holding that most internal heterodox disputes are **not** of this sort.

## Conclusions

- The pluralist paradigm is **demanding**: it needs pluralist economists content and flexible enough to work in a pluralist tradition — carefully comparing approaches, recognising similarities and complementarities, holding a "patiently pragmatic stance" on potential contradictions without ignoring them — and practical training to establish pluralist thinking as scientific practice (the article's main concern).
- Preliminary work exists (Lee 2010's overview of ecumenical/conjoining contributions), but most compare only two or three approaches — much room remains. Intensified work could produce more **"hybrid scholars"** (O'Hara 2008) — transmission belts between schools.
- Look beyond traditional cleavages to novel developments in non-traditional arenas: **economic geography** (itself paradigmatically contested, Boschma and Frenken 2006; its papers heavily reference heterodox journals but not vice versa, Kapeller 2010a) and **behavioural economics** (dissenting in character, paradigmatically contested per Frankfurter and McGoun 2002, positioned orthogonally to traditional cleavages).
- Practice: create settings where pluralism is practiced in a cordial environment — e.g., **cross-school commentary sections** in pluralistically oriented journals to discuss similarities, complementarities and differences, raise awareness of other approaches, and disentangle school-specific biases in reviewing (Hopkins 2010), channelling friction constructively.
- Major assets of the pluralist paradigm as framework for interested pluralism:
  1. **Synthesis of the "solved puzzles"** of different traditions in a single corpus — a competitor to neoclassical economics with greater potential for empirical explanation than any strand alone (independent of whether change comes via revolution or piecemeal evolution, as in the Taylor-rule case).
  2. **A much broader network of journals and outlets** — a greater citation network; given current quality-assessment tendencies this is a "strategic imperative for the survival of diversity in economic thinking".
  3. **A sole competitor to neoclassical economics** — given what we know about paradigms and their historical development, likely a precondition for fundamental long-run change in economics.

## Relation to Proxyeconomics and the Project

- Direct continuation of Dobusch and Kapeller (2009): where the earlier paper diagnosed **citation metrics as failing proxies for research quality**, this paper designs the counter-framework. The pluralist paradigm's evaluative dimension ("content-oriented evaluation instead of citation counting", "ecumenical peer review") is explicitly an **anti-Goodhart reform** of the quality proxy.
- The power-differential argument (fragmented competitors strengthen the dominant paradigm; porous heterodox vs tight mainstream citation networks) formalises how **proxy-based selection** (rankings, "outranking") shapes the ecology of scientific schools — the same selection logic John et al. (2023) describe for proxy failure, and the same prestige-signalling logic the "Consequences of Collaboration" project tests for co-author choice.
- The Table 4 typology (identical → integration … contradictory → empirical tests) is a ready-made methodological toolbox for the Mesa model's epistemic landscape: different research traditions can be modelled as agents whose statements relate by these six types, with "interested pluralism" as an institutional regime.
- Testable conjecture: institutional environments enforcing citation-count proxies should measurably reduce cross-tradition engagement (shift from "interested" to "selfish"/"disinterested" pluralism).

## Robustness & Caveats

- **Conceptual-analytical paper**: the framework is a proposal, not a tested intervention; no data of its own (the citation results are imported from Dobusch and Kapeller 2012 and Kapeller 2010a).
- **Normative-descriptive tension**: the "paradigm" concept is deliberately stripped of epistemological content; readers wanting strong incommensurability theses will find the working definition too thin, and the ideal-speech-situation metaphor imports its own (Habermasian) baggage.
- **Uphill-battle asymmetry** acknowledged but unresolved: evenhanded treatment of neoclassical economics is "difficult for many heterodox economists to implement" facing "continual uphill battles" (Samuels 2000); the recommendation to cite less mainstream work may carry career risks the paper cannot offset institutionally.
- **The Table 4 mapping** is illustrative; the boundaries between (identical/convergent/compatible/neutral/divergent/contradictory) are themselves theory-laden judgments.
- **Exceptions noted**: most heterodox practice remains selfish/disinterested pluralism; the practical uptake of the framework is assumed, not demonstrated.
