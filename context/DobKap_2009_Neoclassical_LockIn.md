**Leonhard Dobusch and Jakob Kapeller**  
*Journal of Economic Issues*, Vol. XLIII No. 4, December 2009, pp. 867–898. DOI: 10.2753/JEI0021-3624430403

## Core Claim

The **paradigmatic dominance of neoclassical economics** is best explained as a **path dependent lock-in**: three positive feedback mechanisms (coordination/network effects, complementarity, and learning effects) create strong barriers to paradigmatic change, and actors strategically reinforce these mechanisms via distinct **"amplifiers"** (win prestigious prizes, close disciplinary borders, assimilate enemies). Citation metrics function as a **"critical juncture"** that institutionalised the dominance already in place. Breaking the lock-in requires a **pluralist (meta-)paradigm** as a single unified competitor to neoclassical economics.

## Reframing Veblen's Question

- Veblen (1898) asked why economics is not an *evolutionary science* — criticising classical theory as purely static and as "a projection of an ideal of conduct" serving as "a canon of truth", and castigating deterministic methods that rule out causalities of economic change, which are "always in the last resort a change in habits of thought".
- Dobusch and Kapeller shift the question: not whether the building blocks of (neo)classical theory follow evolutionary criteria, but **whether economics as a scientific discipline itself evolves over time** — a question with a paradigmatic dimension, about qualitative change in the scientific enterprise.
- The theoretical lens — path dependence theory (David 1985; Sterman and Wittenberg 1999) — is itself the strand of economics that owes most to Veblen (his "systemic obsolescence" and the British railway gauge lock-in, Veblen 1915).

## Why Economics is Locked into Neoclassical Thinking

### Defining "paradigm" and "neoclassical"

- Neoclassical dominance is hard to overlook: nearly every (under)graduate textbook is neoclassical (Lee and Keen 2004 list over 70); mainstream journals, meetings and departments dominate; non-mainstream topics are scarce, especially for newcomers (Michie, Oughton and Wilkinson 2002).
- Following Backhouse (2000), the authors separate orthodoxy from heterodoxy via three criteria — (1) self-identification, (2) sociology (journals, conferences, associations), (3) core beliefs — and modify the third to **core metaphors** (machine, equilibrium, rationality; Mirowski 1989; Lakoff and Johnson 1980).
- Self-identification is dropped as a criterion for defining *mainstream* economists: "normal" economics is directly equated with neoclassical method and style, so mainstream economists see themselves as just "economists", while heterodox economists (marginalised) identify strongly with their approach.
- Core theoretical propositions read as **metaphors** are constitutive of the paradigm's axiomatic foundations; axiomatic variation does not abandon the metaphors — it often strengthens them through broader applicability. Like the Ptolemaic earth-centred metaphor, economic core metaphors carry philosophical, political and ethical implications beyond science.

### Is "neoclassical" still an adequate label?

- Colander (2000); Colander, Holt and Rosser (2004) argue the term is outdated: (a) new programs partially contradicting neoclassical assumptions are accepted in the mainstream, (b) many prominent economists are not "completely" orthodox, (c) today's economics differs from 1950s–60s neoclassicism — leaving only "methodological narrowness" (Dow 2000; Lawson 2006).
- The authors object: new mainstream programs (experimental economics, asymmetric information) start from standard neoclassical assumptions and only modify single axioms. In Kuhnian terms they generate **new puzzles**, not a paradigm change: relaxing perfect rationality or full information opens new *fields of application* for neoclassical theory and method — **axiomatic variation**. Even Colander, Holt and Rosser concede the mainstream is open to approaches "done with a careful understanding of the strengths of the recent orthodox approach".
- Acceptance of experimental economics is conditional on orientation: if it uses mainstream methods and does not claim to falsify/revolutionise the corpus (Sent 2004; Samuelson 2005), its welcome is courteous. Behavioural results (Kahneman, Diener and Schwarz 1999; Layard 2006) do challenge the standard model of behaviour, yet the standard model keeps functioning as the **focal reference point**.
- The same applies to "unorthodox" economists inside the mainstream: in paradigmatic terms, a partially dissenting economist is simply part of the paradigm he works in — a technical distinction.

### Anomalies, Kuhn and Lakatos

- The last 100 years show (neo)classical dominance, though the first 50 do not justify the word *lock-in* (US Institutionalism was an equal competitor; a Keynesian interlude mid-century). The Keynesian "temporal paradigm shift" was possible due to the Great Depression plus a near-complete alternative theory (Keynes 1936).
- Since the *General Theory*, neoclassical economists have "closed the ranks": Keynesian theory was re-integrated into a neoclassical framework (New Keynesianism, micro-foundations of macroeconomics), so that "restrictive monetary and fiscal policies were in fact a regression to the pre-Keynesian orthodoxies of the 1920s" (Michie, Oughton and Wilkinson 2002); Fusfeld (2000) notes today's orthodoxy again ignores the structure of wealth and power.
- Kuhn grants a challenged paradigm three options regarding anomalies: (a) solve them within the paradigm, (b) archive them for better-equipped future generations, (c) partially withdraw the theory. Modern economics does **none** of these: known anomalies (Sraffa 1926; Stigler 1957; Albert [1963] 1971; Kahneman and Tversky 1974; Keen 2001; Ziliak and McCloskey 2008) remain unsolved (rules out a); the theory is still "in power" and praised for political relevance (rules out c); the anomalies are not archived but **ignored** (rules out b).
- Root cause: economic theories are "as-if" theories with extensive ceteris paribus clauses (Friedman [1953] 1966) and a fictitious core (Vaihinger [1911] 1986), making them immune to falsification. Since Popperian falsification is a precondition for recognising an anomaly at all, neoclassical economists **do not recognise** falsifying observations as anomalies (Kapeller 2008).
- Lakatos (1970) corrected this blind spot with "inner core" (fictitious, unfalsifiable) vs "protective belt" (falsifiable auxiliary hypotheses) — but gave no criterion for when enough auxiliary hypotheses fail to constitute an anomaly, so paradigm choice is left to individual scientists or social convention (Chalmers 1989, 87–88).
- Conclusion: Kuhn/Lakatos need to be operationalised with a **mid-range theoretical concept** — path dependence (Sterman and Wittenberg 1999 already did this).

### Path dependence as the mid-range concept

- David (1985) and Arthur (1989) brought path dependence into economics; Sydow, Schreyögg and Koch (2005, 19) define it as a situation "in which an individual actor or a group of actors have lost (at least partly) their power to choose among alternatives because a path reproduces a certain pattern of decisional behavior".
- Read against the described state of the discipline, economics — at least since the 1970s — qualifies as "locked into the neoclassical paradigm". As David (2001) puts it: "I am unable to find any compelling reasons why economic analysis should remain 'locked in' to an ahistorical conceptual framework."

## Emergence of Neoclassical Dominance as Enhanced Path Creation

### Marginalism and early dominance

- Theoretical starting point: the 1870s — Walras (formal theory of markets), Jevons (utilitarian decision-making), Menger (marginal values) (Screpanti and Zamagni 1995). Initially purely theoretical, without institutional power, but attractive through mechanistic elegance.
- By the early 20th century pure economic theory presented itself as a "doctrinal corpus" (Screpanti and Zamagni 1995, 177–178). Divergent arguments were marginalised: Robbins (1928) denounced Marshall's "representative firm" — a heretic, evolutionary element in Marshall's *Principles* — as "positively misleading"; even socialist Oskar Lange (1935) had to "prove" planning efficiency in neoclassical form (the auctioneer as ministry of economic affairs).
- From the 1920s onward neoclassical economics became the dominant habit of thought (Keynes 1936, xii: "The difficulty lies, not in the new ideas, but in escaping from the old ones").

### Post-war renaissance and path formation

- After WWII the community was more heterogeneous (emigration spread schools; Keynesian macro influence; US institutionalism), but path-shaping contributions — Popper's *Poverty of Historicism* (1957), Hayek's *Road to Serfdom* (1944), Friedman's *Essays in Positive Economics* ([1953] 1966), Samuelson's textbook (1948), Arrow–Debreu's military-financed welfare analysis (1954) — put neoclassical economics back on track (Ötsch 2008).
- In path dependence terms this is a **process of path formation**: open-ended, "non-ergodic" (David 1985), with multiple ex-ante unpredictable equilibria — a struggle for the right path. It allows **"intentional path creation"** — "powerful agents ally in order to generate the momentum necessary to create a new ... path" (Sydow, Schreyögg and Koch 2005, 31; cf. Garud and Karnøe 2003 on distributed agency).
- Neoclassical economics had such an organised group: the **Mont Pelerin Society (MPS)** and affiliates, openly committed to confronting "prevailing socialism" (MPS 1947 statement of aims) with an essentially propagandistic motive — one already "knew 'the truth'", the mission "was to evangelize" (Anthony Fisher, cited in Cockett 1995).
- The MPS network grew across academia, media, industry and politics: beyond university departments it built privately funded **think tanks** (Figure 1 charts the growth of MPS-related think tanks and countries from the 1950s–1990s, data from Walpen 2004); Walpen counts over 100 sister organisations (Institute of Economic Affairs, Heritage Foundation, Cato Institute). MPS membership grew from about 170 (1951) to roughly 500 (1991), mostly academics; less than one third work in think tanks — membership numbers are a **poor proxy** for MPS influence, which relied more on academic authority and university teaching.
- Explicit caveat: this is **not a conspiracy argument**. The MPS did not single-handedly cause the neoclassical path (Hayek himself ended in the heterodox Austrian camp); it participated in a principally open path-formation process as an organised, not-negligible group with political interests in exploiting economic theory.
- Complementary independent dynamics: Harvard/MIT scholars rejected MPS politics but agreed on neoclassical theory's primacy (Samuelson 2009 on Friedman vs Hayek); weaknesses of non-mainstream research (e.g., old institutionalism's poorly founded "instinct" analysis, Ayres 1958) discredited alternatives. Backhouse (2005) lists the "histories that can be written": ideology (MPS), learning from mistakes, changed demand for ideas (1970s stagflation), academic entrepreneurship, important individuals, internal dynamics of the profession. This **variety of histories** — emergent *and* strategic — is exactly the contingency phase of path dependence (Sydow, Schreyögg and Koch 2005) and the reason for its ex-ante unpredictability. Hence the emergence of the neoclassical paradigm as dominant path is a case of **enhanced path creation**.

## A Simple Framework: Mechanisms and Amplifiers

Following Sydow, Schreyögg and Koch (2005) and Sterman and Wittenberg (1999), most path dependence scholars identify three mechanism categories that can produce lock-in:

| Mechanism | Definition | Typical association |
|-----------|------------|---------------------|
| Coordination / direct network effects | Utility of adopting a technology/behaviour rises with the number of adopters (e.g., driving on the right side) (North 1990; Ackermann 2001) | Increasing returns to scale (Arthur 1996) |
| Complementarity / indirect network effects | Complementary practices/institutions raise the utility of adoption (Dosi 1982; Katz and Shapiro 1985; e.g., software variety makes computers more useful) | Increasing returns to scale (Shapiro and Varian 1999) |
| Learning / irreversible investments | Adopters accumulate specific, non-transferable knowledge; updating beats switching (Williamson 1985; Arthur 1996) | Sunk costs (Arrow 2003) |

- **Amplifiers**: the label "mechanism" implies logic working independently of actor strategies, but strategic and exogenous interests shape the mechanisms' functioning. Observations that are not part of the mechanisms themselves but reinforce them are *amplifiers*; the mechanisms are the precondition making the amplifiers more than ordinary strategic action. Given economics' impact on societal hegemony in economic policy, these are also **"hegemonial strategies"** (focus here on amplifiers; potential "dampers" only in the outlook).

## Mechanism #1: Increasing Returns and Network Effects in Economics

- Working inside the neoclassical paradigm has significant institutional advantages: jobs, research funding, publication chances, curricula, supervisors (Earl 1983). Joining the neoclassical community yields immediate benefits — a direct network effect with "self-reinforcing feedback processes" (Sterman and Wittenberg 1999).
- Sociologically: "mutual dependence" — "economists generally have to adhere to a dominant neoclassical strategic paradigm to be taken seriously" (Siler 2003). Empirical backing: roughly one quarter of authors include statements they would normally reject to please reviewers (Bedeian 2003); 60% of North-American economists agree a "good-old-boy" network influences article acceptance (Davis 2007). Consensus shifts toward monetarist/new classical propositions accordingly (Fuller and Geide-Stevenson 2003; Frey, Humbert and Schneider 2007 for German-speaking countries).
- Theoretical increasing returns: decades of collective deepening gave neoclassical theory a "starting advantage" and surprisingly high apparent explanatory power — analogous to the Ptolemaic model outpredicting early heliocentrism (Kuhn [1962] 1996). Real Business Cycle theory fits data closely only through "econometric creativity": the residual between model and data is reinterpreted as technological change and reinserted into the model — astonishing accuracy, little relevance (King and Rebelo 2000).
- **Puzzle creation through axiomatic variation is a strategic advantage**: modifying one or two axioms (e.g., Multiple-Self theory adding "curious inducements" to the homo oeconomicus utility function to "explain" altruism, environmentalism, discrimination) continuously delivers new "unsolved puzzles", makes "the system more attractive" (Dobusch and Schüßler 2007) and easier to defend against critique.
- The neoclassical network is the only route to the top of the discipline: top journals, the Nobel Prize, and international policy institutions all effectively require neoclassical commitment as a "minimum condition" for recognition.

### Amplifier #1: Get the prize

- The Nobel Prize in economics is quasi-neoclassical for three reasons: (1) it is the "Bank of Sweden Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel", funded and governed by the Bank of Sweden, not Nobel's heritage; (2) prize committee chairmen (Erik Lundberg, later Assar Lindbeck) were MPS members/associates and free-market advocates; (3) its implementation was an MPS-organised project to promote neoclassical ideas (Ötsch 2008).
- The prize thus serves as a strategic instrument of intellectual hegemony — "a barrier for new thinking" (Söderbaum 2004). Very few laureates are heterodox; the claim that the Nobel furthered pluralism is hard to defend.

## Mechanism #2: Complementary Institutions and Collateral Consequences

- Across research, quality is increasingly measured by quantitative indexation. In economics the **simultaneous rise of neoclassical dominance and citation indexing** produced mutually reinforcing legitimation of both systems.
- The **Matthew Effect** (Merton 1968): the already-recognised receive more recognition — working for researchers (Nobel laureates even refuse co-authorship to protect associates' credit) and for journals: better reputation → more prominent submissions → better reviewers → more readers and citations → higher reputation. The "big eight" universities (Berkeley, Harvard, Stanford, Yale, Chicago, Columbia, Princeton, MIT) dominate the boards of top journals and supply many of their authors.
- Hodgson and Rothman (1999) call this "institutional oligopoly" with explicit path dependence: initial concentrations of editors/authors trigger positive feedback (grants, visibility, recruiting, output). From the 1960s, "objective" measures (citation indexes, Thomson's Journal Impact Factor, the A–E journal categorisation) institutionalised and reinforced the dynamic: "A" journals reproduce their top position; a stable mutually citing cluster makes it quasi-impossible for new or dissident journals to compete. Top-journal publication is crucial for careers, departmental reputation and financing (Lee 2008b; footnote: the Notre Dame economics department split, explicitly justified by ranking logic, Hayes 2007).

### Amplifier #2: Close your borders

- Neoclassical proponents define the borders of the discipline: heterodox schools, qualitative methods and socio-economic approaches are excluded and labelled "sociology", "not representative" or "esoteric" — regardless of subject matter. "One of the privileges bestowed upon those who adhere the conventional paradigm is that they are not required to defend the very foundation of their analysis" (Whalen 1987).
- Episodes: Samuelson denied Galbraith the profession of economist; the *Journal of Political Economy* rejected Joan Robinson's capital-controversy paper, suggesting "a journal for the amateur intellectual or ... an obscure journal" (Lee 2004); post-autistic-economics students were attacked as bad mathematicians (Solow 2001); Leijonhufvud's (1973) satire: the Economist dismisses sociologists and political scientists because "they do not make models".
- Interdisciplinary concepts face scepticism to open hostility ("the exclusivism and exclusionism which they practice ... by equating economics with neoclassicism", Samuels 2000); interdisciplinary research "is not worth its opportunity costs, because you can publish it nowhere" (quoted department member). Conversely, **economic imperialism** (Lazear 2000) applies the narrow method to non-economic problems, converting them into new puzzles for the dominant paradigm. The strategy is not to restrict the applicability of economic thinking but to **control which people and arguments are admitted** to the discourse.

## Mechanism #3: Learning Effects

- Learning effects operate on **both supply and demand sides**. Supply side: a stable, logically consistent body of knowledge plus a standard method set fulfilling "ceremonial adequacy" (Veblen 1898) defines what one must know to enter the community — a strategic advantage over social sciences lacking an obligatory theoretical core (lower cohesion).
- The standard axiom/method complex functions as **"asset specificity"** (Williamson 1985): past intellectual investment must not be wasted — the scientist "defends his assets, mostly consisting of knowledge drummed into the economist's head during his graduate studies and repeated in slight modifications till retirement" (Peukert 2006). As a "labor saving device", updating the standard is easier than learning something new.
- Socialisation: "The young Econ[omist], or 'grad,' is not admitted to adulthood until he has made a 'model' exhibiting a degree of workmanship acceptable to the elders of the 'dept'" (Leijonhufvud 1973). Younger economists show disproportionately high agreement with neoclassical policy relevance (Frey, Humbert and Schneider 2007). Excessive "adaptiveness" yields a noncritical research culture of "doing their things well without knowing (on a meta-level) what they are doing" — the paradigm reproduces itself via prestige, career prospects, citation oligopolies and the ambition of the young (Earl 1983: the wisest strategy "may be to be a neoclassical economist too").
- Demand side: a "light" version of neoclassical theory (Mankiw-type textbooks) is non-formal, easy to grasp and delivered to mass audiences (students, laypeople, politicians). Its metaphors often carry a free-market ideological taint (Ötsch 2008) but are easy to follow in practice once utility/profit maximisation, equilibrium, supply-and-demand and Pareto-efficiency are understood (note 27: "profit maximization" is anything but easy in practice).

### Amplifier #3: Assimilate your enemies

- Every heterodox branch has a quarrelsome relation to neoclassicism and a unique selling proposition (evolutionary economics: economic change; ecological economics: the environment; feminist economics: gender relations). The mainstream accepts those contributions that fit neoclassical implementation: Pigou's externalities revitalised as environmental economics; utility maximisation and public choice adapted to institutions (neo-institutionalism); Becker's (1971) economic analysis of discrimination. Some heterodox ideas enter the mainstream — others do not.
- Schumpeter is popular in neoclassical growth/cycle theory (Barro and Sala-i-Martin 2004; Helpman and Trajtenberg 1994; Aghion 1998), but reduced to "creative destruction" justifying technology jumps or diffusion between equilibria — contradicting Schumpeter's core insight that "there is no dynamic equilibrium ... Development and Equilibrium ... are antagonistic and cancelling each other out" (Schumpeter [1912] 2006; note 28: attributing "punctuated equilibrium" to Schumpeter misreads his criticism of equilibrium approaches).
- Keynes: mathematicised by Samuelson and Hicks, who "left out all those bits that were inconsistent with the neoclassical axioms ... a formalised version of Keynes that is like a Henry Miller novel without sex and profanity" (Fulbrook 2007). Hicks's (1937) IS-LM "book review" summarised Keynes inside the neoclassical framework while withholding imperfect markets, liquidity preference, expectations and uncertainty — "Keynes without uncertainty is something like Hamlet without the prince" (Minsky [1975] 2008). By symmetry: "Schumpeter without history is rather like Keynes without uncertainty". Hicks himself asked whether the revision is "a sham fight" (Hicks 1937).
- Mechanism: assimilate only the parts compatible with the existing corpus, then claim the research area is already covered and the alternative is "not so new anyway". Whether conscious or emergent, **assimilation closes ranks and a priori prevents path-breaking behaviour**.

## Conclusions and Remarks

Summary of results:

- (a) Path dependence theory explains Kuhnian paradigmatic dominance as a **theoretical lock-in** arising from positive feedback mechanisms within a field.
- (b) Economics is locked into neoclassical thinking, preventing alternatives regardless of quality — the reason for its parochial, non-pluralistic character.
- (c) The lock-in is reinforced by hegemonial strategies; **citation metrics as "objective" quality measure constitute a critical juncture** (Mahoney 2000) that institutionalised the existing dominance. Concepts of path dependence (economics), hegemony (political science) and paradigm (philosophy of science) are notably similar (Sterman and Wittenberg 1999).

### Path-breaking strategies (Sydow, Schreyögg and Koch 2005)

- **Discursive strategies**: surface and reflect hidden self-reinforcing patterns. Blind spots of standard theory: questionable epistemological grounding (Vaihinger [1911] 1986; Friedman [1953] 1966), absence of qualitative methods, the monopoly of one theory. Heterodox schools should highlight these blind spots in all contexts (publications, lectures, conferences, student publications, public speeches) and encourage reflexive self-examination; tackling politically salient problems can be a "back-door option" into the economic debate. Note the unwritten rule: "it is bad to spend time on 'methodological' and broad-stroke issues" (Rabin 2002).
- **Behavioral strategies**: overcome unconscious routines. Two hindrances: departments obey the elders (Leijonhufvud's "villages" and "perish in the wilderness"); and the "economic method" — "rather than starting with a question ... modern economists usually start with a particular type of method and presume, mistakenly, that it must be appropriate to all social contexts" (Lawson 2004). Unlocking economics requires changing methodological membership rules; diversified methods favour heterodox theories; alliances with methodologically open disciplines can broaden debate.
- **Citation tactics** for heterodox researchers: cite working-paper versions of orthodox articles, abstain from redundant orthodox citations; heterodox editors should increase "domestic production of citations" (Lee 2008b) — while remembering that metric games cannot compensate for lack of research quality.
- **Systemic approaches**: attend to autopoietic reproduction of social systems. In-between programs (experimental, evolutionary economics) face an **attribution problem**: their results are read as enrichments — "solved puzzles" — of neoclassical theory rather than as the anomalies and falsifications they substantially are (sometimes because authors themselves relate findings to the dominant paradigm, Rabin 2002; Fehr and Gächter 2000). Since paradigm strength depends on the ratio of solved to unsolved puzzles (Kuhn; Sterman and Wittenberg 1999), "enriching" neoclassicism strategically strengthens it; trying to "preserve most of the discipline's significant achievements" (Nelson and Winter [1982] 1996) is shortsighted.

### Competing paradigm or pluralism?

- If a competing paradigm is a precondition for the withdrawal of a dominant one (Kuhn), should heterodoxy present itself as a competing paradigm or plead for pluralism (Van Bouwel 2005; King 2004)?
- Formal models of scientific revolutions (Sterman and Wittenberg 1999) show the **number of competing paradigms** (relative to the dominant one) is more decisive than intrinsic quality: more competitors → lower probability of paradigm shift, higher probability that competitors perish as "foolish errors of the past".
- Therefore **theoretical pluralism has the strategic advantage of a unified paradigmatic alternative**: a joint pluralist (meta-)paradigm integrates the strengths of many schools, accumulates their "puzzles solved", and enables common curricula, journals, textbooks and doctoral programs.
- Closing claim: paradigmatic dominance is not the inevitable fate of science but a **result of social interactions** — and since it is social, it can be escaped by deconstructing our social reality and insisting on theoretical and methodical pluralism as the central guideline of economic research.

## Relation to Proxyeconomics and the Project

- The paper is a sustained case study of **proxy failure inside science**: citation counts, impact factors and journal rankings are proxies for "research quality" whose self-reinforcing gaming (Matthew Effect, network closure, citation transfer asymmetry) entrenches a paradigm — an early companion to the Goodhart/Campbell logic of John et al. (2023).
- Mechanisms map onto the proxy framework: the citation metric is the proxy $P$; intellectual quality/progress is the goal $G$; the "amplifiers" are deliberate pressure strategies on $P$ (prize allocation, boundary policing, assimilation), exactly the regulatory pressure John et al. describe.
- Institutional relevance for the "Consequences of Collaboration" project: prestige signals (Nobel, top-eight departments, top journals) act as proxies for intellectual merit — the same structure as prestigious co-authors standing in for research quality. The paper documents how such prestige networks become self-reproducing (tight orthodox citation core, $64.22\%$ intra-network citations) — a template for studying rank-homophily in co-authorship.
- The 2012 companion paper (Dobusch and Kapeller, "Heterodox United vs. Mainstream City?") develops the constructive counter-proposal — a "pluralist (meta-)paradigm" — sketched at the end of this article.

## Robustness & Caveats

- **Historical-descriptive, not formal**: the path dependence framework is applied heuristically; no model is estimated, and the 2009 citation analysis is illustrative ($n=20$ journals per camp).
- **Data limitations**: SSCI-based citation data partially discriminates against heterodox journals (many are unlisted; note 26); the authors compensate by comparing "ten of every branch" but acknowledge residual biases.
- **The MPS narrative risks over-reading**: the authors explicitly reject conspiracy framing and stress complementary, partly emergent dynamics ("variety of histories"); the think-tank network size is itself only a "proxy" for MPS influence.
- **Amplifier taxonomy is heuristic**: mechanisms and amplifiers overlap empirically (the authors admit learning vs coordination is partly a labelling question) and dampers are not systematically treated.
- **Strategic recommendations are speculative**: citation tactics and meta-paradigm formation are argued plausibly but not tested.
